\epigraph{%
  Electric charge is not a substance lodged in the interior of matter.\\
  It is a topological fact about the boundary between\\
  one partition region and the next.%
}{}

The preceding chapters established mass as a property of the \emph{interior}
of a Koopman eigenmode --- the rest frequency of its intrinsic oscillation.
This chapter establishes that charge is instead a property of
\emph{boundaries}: it is defined on the partition boundary separating two
regions of $\Omega$, and is undefined in the interior of any single partition
region.  The result is proved by five independent arguments.  The chapter
closes by deriving exact charge quantisation from the binary character of
partition boundaries.

\section{Charge Emergence from Partition Boundaries}
\label{sec:chargeemergence}

Electric charge is usually taken as a primitive property of matter, defined
by its coupling to the electromagnetic field.  In the partition framework,
charge is not primitive: it is a structural attribute of partition boundaries.
The following consequence establishes this with five independent proofs.

\begin{consequence}{Charge is a Boundary Property}{chargeemergence}
Let $B$ be a partition boundary: a measurable set of $\mu$-measure zero that
separates two positive-measure regions of $\Omega$.  The electric charge $q$
associated with crossing $B$ is well-defined on $B$; it is not well-defined
in the interior of a single partition region.
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
We give five independent arguments.

\emph{(a) Operational.}  By Consequence~\ref{cons:categorical}, every
measurement resolves a partition.  To measure charge is to register a discrete
category of the apparatus.  A single interior point of a partition region is
not separated from its neighbourhood by a boundary; there is nothing for the
apparatus to register.  Hence charge is not operationally accessible in the
interior.

\emph{(b) Internal neutrality.}  Suppose a composite system $\mathcal{C}$
consists of constituents $\{c_{i}\}$ that share the partition structure of
$\mathcal{C}$'s envelope (Consequence~\ref{cons:bindingenergy}).  Then no
partition boundary between individual $c_{i}$ survives; by (a), no individual
charge assignment is meaningful.  The composite as a whole has a charge
assigned to its envelope boundary, which does survive.  Internal
constituents of a composite are therefore individually uncharged in the
operational sense; only the envelope boundary carries a definite charge.

\emph{(c) Boundedness.}  Let a composite have $Z$ constituents each with a
putative charge $\pm 1$.  Tracking every pairwise charge assignment requires
$Z!$ orderings; the compression cost of storing all $Z!$ distinguishable
orderings in $\Omega$ is, by Consequence~\ref{cons:compression}, divergent as
$Z! \to \infty$.  Axiom~\ref{ax:bpsl}(i) forbids this.  Hence interior
charges are not tracked individually; only the boundary charge --- a single
number assigned to the composite envelope --- is tracked.

\emph{(d) Creation by partitioning.}  Splitting a bound composite across a
new partition boundary creates two regions that individually carry net
boundary charges, even if the composite as a whole is neutral.  The charges
were not present in the interior prior to the split; they emerged at the new
boundaries.  This explains the observation that ionisation of a neutral atom
produces positive and negative fragments.

\emph{(e) Analogy.}  ``Wet'' is a boundary property: a submerged solid object
is surrounded by water but its interior is not wet; only its outer surface is.
Analogously, ``charged'' is a boundary property of a partition region; the
interior is neither charged nor uncharged.

Each argument independently concludes that charge is defined on boundaries,
not in interiors.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The five arguments compared}

The five independent arguments differ in generality and in the type of
conclusion each supports.
\begin{itemize}
\item Argument (a), operational, is the strongest in that it is independent
  of any specific physical model: if measurement is a partition operation
  (Consequence~\ref{cons:categorical}), then what is measured must be a
  partition attribute.
\item Argument (b), internal neutrality, is the most structurally informative:
  it explains why composite systems can have net zero charge while having
  charged constituents in the boundary regime.
\item Argument (c), boundedness, invokes Axiom~\ref{ax:bpsl}(i) directly; it
  is the most directly tied to the Axiom.
\item Argument (d), creation by partitioning, is the strongest empirical
  statement: it explains the observation that breaking bound systems produces
  charged fragments.
\item Argument (e), the ``wet'' analogy, is pedagogical and supports
  intuition.
\end{itemize}
All five arguments converge on the same conclusion.  This convergence is not
redundancy; it is structural robustness.

\subsection{Observational test: charge on a conductor}

A neutral conducting sphere has no observable charge.  Remove some electrons;
the sphere now carries net positive charge $+Ne$ on its surface (not
volumetrically distributed through the interior).  The charge resides on the
boundary, not in the interior, exactly as Consequence~\ref{cons:chargeemergence}
predicts.

Maxwell's equations enforce that $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside a conductor in
equilibrium, i.e., the gradient of $\Depth$ vanishes inside.  The charge
must reside on the boundary.  This is the continuum-limit statement of
Consequence~\ref{cons:chargeemergence}.

\section{Exact Quantisation of Charge}
\label{sec:chargequant}

\begin{consequence}{Charge Quantisation}{chargequantisation}
The electric charge of any partition boundary takes integer multiples of a
fundamental unit $e > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  q \in \{0,\; \pm e,\; \pm 2e,\; \ldots\} = e\,\mathbb{Z}.
  \label{eq:chargequant}
\end{equation}
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
By Consequence~\ref{cons:chargeemergence}, charge is a property of a partition
boundary.  By Axiom~\ref{ax:bpsl}(iii), a partition boundary is either present
or absent between two cells: it is a binary attribute.  The fundamental unit
of charge $e$ is the charge associated with a single presence-absence
transition of a boundary.  The charge of a region with $N$ boundary
transitions is $Ne$, with $N \in \mathbb{Z}$ (the sign encodes the direction
of the transition: entering versus leaving the region).

No intermediate values $e/2$, $e/3$, etc., are permitted, because the
partition boundary itself is not divisible into sub-boundaries of partial
presence.  A boundary either separates two cells or it does not; there is
no half-separation.  Hence charge is quantised in integer multiples of $e$,
with no intermediate values.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Millikan's 1909 oil-drop experiment \citep{millikan1913} measures exactly
this quantisation, finding that charge comes only in integer multiples of a
fundamental unit.  The modern best value $e = 1.602\,176\,634 \times 10^{-19}$~C
\citep{codata2018} fixes the scale of $e$ in terms of the partition topology
of electromagnetic bound states.  No additional postulate is needed beyond
the Axiom for the existence of charge quantisation; the integer character of
$N$ follows from the binary character of partition boundaries.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Fractional charges in sub-nuclear physics}

The confinement of quarks within hadrons corresponds to partition cells that
are not fully resolved in the exterior --- a regime in which the boundary
transitions are path-opaque rather than path-transparent.  In this regime,
what appears as a fractional charge $e/3$ or $2e/3$ from outside the hadronic
boundary is an integer charge $e$ or $2e$ of the full boundary, projected
onto the observation frame that cannot resolve the interior transitions.
The observed fractional charges do not contradict~\eqref{eq:chargequant};
they are a consequence of the coarser resolution available outside the
hadronic envelope.  Chapter~\ref{ch:subnucleon} treats this in detail.

\section{Gauge Structure and the Coulomb Field}
\label{sec:ch8-gauge}

The boundary character of charge is consistent with, and in fact explains,
the gauge structure of electromagnetism.

\subsection{Gauge invariance from the scalar-vector coupling}

The partition Lagrangian (Chapter~\ref{ch:lagrangian}) contains both a scalar
coupling $-\Depth$ and a vector coupling $\mu\dot{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\Apartition$.
As noted in the Appendix to Part II of the source paper, this Lagrangian is
invariant under
\[
  \Apartition \to \Apartition + \nabla\chi,
  \qquad
  \Depth \to \Depth - \partial_{t}(\mu\chi),
\]
for any smooth function $\chi$.  This is the $U(1)$ gauge symmetry of
electromagnetism.  It emerges here not as an imposed symmetry but as a
consequence of the scalar-plus-vector coupling structure: any function that
can be shifted without affecting the trajectories (i.e., without affecting
the physical observables, which are boundary crossings) is a gauge freedom.

\subsection{The Coulomb field as the partition depth gradient}

In the static case, the equation of motion derived from the partition
Lagrangian reduces to $\mu\ddot{\mathbf{x}} = -\nabla\Depth$.  The partition
depth field $\Depth(\mathbf{x})$ is divergence-free in the bulk of a partition
region (no sources in the interior, by Consequence~\ref{cons:chargeemergence}).
The unique spherically symmetric solution to $\nabla^{2}\Depth = 0$ is
$\Depth \propto 1/r$, giving
\[
  \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) := -\nabla\Depth(\mathbf{r}) \propto \frac{1}{r^{2}},
\]
the Coulomb field.  Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \rho/\varepsilon_{0}$
is the continuum-limit statement that charge (source of $\mathbf{E}$) resides
on boundaries (where $\Depth$ is discontinuous and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}$
is singular).

\begin{remark}
The electromagnetic field is not a separate physical entity in this framework.
It is the spatial extension of the partition structure: the gradient of the
partition depth field in regions accessible to a bounded chain of partition
boundaries.  This is compatible with all electromagnetic observations but
removes the conceptual need for a ``field as substance''.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Dimensional analysis}

The partition Lagrangian $\Lagr_{\Depth}$ has dimensions of energy
$[\mathrm{M\,L}^{2}\mathrm{T}^{-2}]$.  The kinetic term
$\tfrac{1}{2}\mu|\dot{\mathbf{x}}|^{2}$ has dimensions $[\mu][\mathrm{L}^{2}\mathrm{T}^{-2}]
= \mathrm{M\,L}^{2}\mathrm{T}^{-2}$ (energy).  The vector coupling term
$\mu\dot{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\Apartition$ requires $[\Apartition] = \mathrm{L\,T}^{-1}$
(velocity).  The scalar coupling $\Depth$ has dimensions of energy.  These
match the electromagnetic scalar and vector potentials (with appropriate unit
choice), confirming that the partition Lagrangian has the same dimensional
structure as the electromagnetic coupling, with no free parameters beyond the
identification of $\Depth$ with the electrostatic potential and $\Apartition$
with the magnetic vector potential.
