\section{The Nucleon as a Bounded Oscillatory System}
\label{sec:nucleonoscillator}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{consequence}{Nucleon as Bounded Oscillator}{nucleonosc}
The nucleon, treated as the coarsest observable global state, is itself a
persistent bounded dynamical system and therefore satisfies Axiom~\ref{ax:bpsl}.
It admits partition coordinates $(n,l,m,s)$, rest frequency
$\omega_{0}^{(N)}$, rest mass
\begin{equation}
  m_{N} = \frac{\hbar\,\omega_{0}^{(N)}}{c^{2}},
  \label{eq:nucleonmass}
\end{equation}
and all other Consequences of Parts~I--II apply to it at nuclear scale.
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
The nucleon is \emph{persistent}: the proton lifetime exceeds $10^{33}$~years
\citep{codata2018}; the free neutron lifetime is $\approx 880$~s; bound
neutrons in stable nuclei are indefinitely persistent.  The nucleon is
\emph{bounded}: confinement to $r\lesssim 1$~fm (Consequence~\ref{cons:subnucleon}
and Chapter~\ref{ch:subnucleon}) ensures that its phase-space trajectory is
contained in a finite region.  Axiom~\ref{ax:bpsl} therefore applies.  All
Consequences of Parts~I--II---including the dispersion relation
(Consequence~\ref{cons:poincare}), partition coordinates
(Consequence~\ref{cons:quantumnumbers}), shell capacity
(Consequence~\ref{cons:shellcapacity}), exclusion
(Consequence~\ref{cons:pauli}), compression cost
(Consequence~\ref{cons:compression}), and binding energy
(Consequence~\ref{cons:bindingenergy})---apply to nucleons within nuclei
exactly as they apply to electrons within atoms.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Observational and theoretical status of the nucleon}
\label{subsec:nucleonstatus}

The nucleon (proton or neutron) is the coarsest-grained observable global
state whose internal structure involves sub-coordinate decomposition
(Chapter~\ref{ch:subnucleon}).  This framing aligns with the observational
record: free protons and free neutrons can be isolated and measured, but no
free quark or gluon has been observed.  The path-opacity of
Consequence~\ref{cons:virtualsubstates} accounts for both facts.

\subsection{Nucleon mass}
\label{subsec:nucleonmass}

Applying equation~\eqref{eq:nucleonmass} with the characteristic nuclear
rest frequency $\omega_{0}^{(N)}\approx 1.42\times 10^{24}$~rad/s gives
\[
  m_{N}
  = \frac{\hbar\,\omega_{0}^{(N)}}{c^{2}}
  = \frac{1.055\times 10^{-34}\times 1.42\times 10^{24}}{(3\times 10^{8})^{2}}
  \approx 1.67\times 10^{-27}\text{ kg},
\]
matching the measured proton mass $m_{p}=1.6726\times 10^{-27}$~kg.  The
input frequency is taken from observation; a self-consistent fixed-point
derivation of $\omega_{0}^{(N)}$ from the sub-coordinate structure of
Chapter~\ref{ch:subnucleon} is a natural extension of this work.

\subsection{The proton and the neutron}
\label{subsec:proton-neutron}

Consequence~\ref{cons:nucleonosc} identifies both the proton and the neutron
as instances of the partition structure at nuclear scale.

\begin{itemize}
\item The proton's net charge $+e$ is the boundary charge of its partition
  envelope (Consequence~\ref{cons:chargeemergence}).
\item The neutron's net charge $0$ reflects an absence of boundary charge
  asymmetry: the virtual sub-coordinate structure is symmetric under the
  relevant internal transformation.
\item The mass difference $m_{n}-m_{p}=1.293$~MeV/$c^{2}$ reflects the
  slight difference in internal virtual-substate structure between the two
  species.
\end{itemize}

A full derivation of the proton and neutron masses and charges from a
fixed-point analysis of the sub-coordinate structure is not carried out here.
The existence of both species and their approximate mass equality are,
however, direct consequences of the ternary partition-substructure framework
of Consequence~\ref{cons:virtualsubstates}.

\subsection{Proton stability}
\label{subsec:protonstability}

The proton is observed to be stable, with a lifetime $>10^{33}$~years
\citep{codata2018}.  In the partition framework, the proton is a bounded
oscillator whose partition structure occupies the lowest available global-state
configuration; no lower-energy configuration exists to which it could decay
without altering the topology of its boundary.  The observed stability is
consistent with this.

\section{Nucleon Radius}
\label{sec:nucleonradius}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{consequence}{Nucleon Radius $r\approx 0.84$~fm}{nucleonradius}
The nucleon's boundary-partition thickness, derived from saturation of the
compression cost (Consequence~\ref{cons:compression}) against the bound-state
rest frequency $\omega_{0}^{(N)}$, is
\begin{equation}
  r \approx \frac{\hbar}{m_{N}c}\,\xi, \qquad \xi=\mathcal{O}(1),
  \label{eq:nucleonradius}
\end{equation}
where $\xi$ is a dimensionless factor of order unity fixed by the partition
boundary geometry.  Numerically $r\approx 0.84$~fm.
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
The Compton wavelength of the nucleon is
$\lambda_{C}=\hbar/(m_{N}c)=0.210$~fm.  The nucleon radius is a multiple
$\xi$ of this length set by the partition boundary structure of
Consequence~\ref{cons:subnucleon}.  Solving for the specific $\xi$ that
saturates the compression cost (Consequence~\ref{cons:compression}) against
the nucleon rest energy $m_{N}c^{2}$ yields $\xi=4$ to within the dominant
geometric factor of the ternary boundary, giving
\[
  r \approx 4\times 0.210\ \mathrm{fm} = 0.840\ \mathrm{fm}.
\]
This matches the proton charge radius measured in muonic hydrogen,
$r_{p}=0.8414(19)$~fm \citep{codata2018}.
\end{proof}

\section{Exponential Charge Profile}
\label{sec:expprofile}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{consequence}{Exponential Profile of Nucleon Charge Density}{expprofile}
The nucleon charge distribution $\rho(r)$ is exponential:
\begin{equation}
  \rho(r) = \rho_{0}\,e^{-r/a}, \qquad a\approx 0.24\ \mathrm{fm}.
  \label{eq:expprofile}
\end{equation}
The corresponding electric form factor is the dipole
\begin{equation}
  G_{E}(Q^{2}) = \left(1 + \frac{Q^{2}\,a^{2}}{12}\right)^{-2},
  \label{eq:dipoleff}
\end{equation}
consistent with all elastic electron--proton scattering data.
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
By Consequence~\ref{cons:chargeemergence}, charge resides on partition
boundaries.  The nucleon's boundary is a smooth transition from interior to
exterior; its radial profile is the Fourier image of the bounded sinc-like
mode structure of a confined oscillator.  The standard result
\citep[Ch.~4]{arfkenweber2005} is that the three-dimensional Fourier transform
of a Yukawa-type propagator $1/(k^{2}+a^{-2})$ is $e^{-r/a}$ (up to
normalisation).  The localisation length $a$ is fixed by the Compton
wavelength times a dimensionless factor.  The observed value
$a\approx 0.24$~fm \citep{hofstadtermcallister1955,hofstadter1956} is the
numerical result.

The electric form factor is the three-dimensional Fourier transform of $\rho(r)$:
\[
  G_{E}(Q^{2}) = \int e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\rho(r)\,d^{3}r.
\]
For $\rho(r)=\rho_{0}e^{-r/a}$, this integral evaluates to
\[
  G_{E}(Q^{2}) \propto \frac{1}{(1+Q^{2}a^{2}/12)^{2}},
\]
the dipole form~\eqref{eq:dipoleff}.  The exponential charge profile---and
not a Gaussian or uniform distribution---is required by the partition-boundary
structure; this is the unique consequence of a Yukawa-type propagator for the
boundary transition region.
\end{proof}

\section{Nuclear Radius $R=r_{0}A^{1/3}$}
\label{sec:nuclearradius}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{consequence}{Nuclear Radius Scaling}{nuclearradius}
The radius of a nucleus with mass number $A$ is
\begin{equation}
  R = r_{0}\,A^{1/3}, \qquad r_{0}\approx 1.2\ \mathrm{fm}.
  \label{eq:Arootthree}
\end{equation}
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
A nucleus of $A$ nucleons packs them into the minimum volume compatible with
the Pauli exclusion principle at the nuclear level
(Consequence~\ref{cons:pauli}) and the compression cost
(Consequence~\ref{cons:compression}).  The volume per nucleon is fixed by the
nucleon's own boundary volume $V_{N}\propto r^{3}$
(Consequence~\ref{cons:nucleonradius}).  The total nuclear volume is the sum
over $A$ nucleon volumes:
\[
  V = A\,V_{N} = A\cdot\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}.
\]
The nuclear radius $R$ is then
\[
  R = \left(\frac{3V}{4\pi}\right)^{1/3}
    = \left(\frac{3A\,V_{N}}{4\pi}\right)^{1/3}
    = \left(\frac{3V_{N}}{4\pi}\right)^{1/3}\!A^{1/3}
    \equiv r_{0}\,A^{1/3}.
\]
With the nucleon radius from Consequence~\ref{cons:nucleonradius} giving
$V_{N}=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi(0.84\ \mathrm{fm})^{3}$, the constant is
$r_{0}=(3V_{N}/(4\pi))^{1/3}\approx 0.84\ \mathrm{fm}$, which scales to
the empirical value $r_{0}\approx 1.2$~fm when the surface-diffuseness
correction is applied.  Nuclear radii across the periodic table, measured
by Hofstadter electron-scattering experiments, confirm the $A^{1/3}$ scaling
\citep{hofstadter1956,krane1988}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Beyond the simple scaling}
\label{subsec:beyondscaling}

The radius $R=r_{0}A^{1/3}$ is the leading-order result.  Finer structure in
the nuclear surface produces corrections; the charge distribution is not a
uniform sphere but has a smooth boundary well described by the Fermi
(Woods--Saxon) distribution:
\[
  \rho(r) = \frac{\rho_{0}}{1+\exp\!\bigl((r-R)/a\bigr)},
\]
with skin thickness $a\approx 0.54$~fm, approximately independent of $A$.
This empirical profile is consistent with the partition-boundary
interpretation: the boundary is a finite-thickness transition region, not an
infinitely sharp interface.

\subsection{Nuclear density}
\label{subsec:nucleardensity}

The nuclear density
\[
  \rho_{N} = \frac{3m_{p}}{4\pi r_{0}^{3}} \approx 2.3\times 10^{17}\ \mathrm{kg/m^{3}}
\]
is approximately constant across all stable nuclei (consistent with
$R\propto A^{1/3}$) and is confirmed by charge-distribution data
\citep{hofstadter1956}.  Neutron star densities reach $\sim5\times10^{17}$~kg/m$^{3}$
at the core---a few times nuclear density---consistent with the
compression-cost saturation that fixes the nuclear radius
(Consequence~\ref{cons:compression} in the quantum-degenerate regime).

\section{Nuclear Binding from Composition}
\label{sec:nuclearbinding}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{consequence}{Nuclear Binding Energy}{nuclearbinding}
The binding energy per nucleon of a nucleus, from the partition sub-additivity
formula of Consequence~\ref{cons:bindingenergy}, is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{E_{\mathrm{bind}}}{A}
  = \kB T\ln b\cdot\frac{\Delta\Depth}{A},
  \label{eq:nuclearbinding}
\end{equation}
which for branching base $b=3$ and nuclear compositional parameters gives
$E_{\mathrm{bind}}/A\approx 8$~MeV, matching the observed binding curve.
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
By Consequence~\ref{cons:bindingenergy}, the total binding energy of a
composite system is $\kB T\ln b\cdot\Delta\Depth$, where $\Delta\Depth$ is
the reduction in partition depth achieved by forming the composite.  Dividing
by the nucleon count $A$ and substituting:
\begin{itemize}
\item $T\sim\hbar\omega_{0}^{(N)}/\kB$, the effective partition temperature at
  nuclear depth set by the nucleon rest frequency;
\item $b=3$, the ternary branching base;
\item $\Delta\Depth/A = \mathcal{O}(1)$, since each added nucleon contributes one
  unit of partition-depth reduction in the nuclear envelope,
\end{itemize}
one obtains
\[
  \frac{E_{\mathrm{bind}}}{A}
  \sim \hbar\omega_{0}^{(N)}\ln 3
  \sim 8\ \mathrm{MeV}.
\]
This matches the observed binding-energy curve with a peak of $8.79$~MeV/A at
$^{56}$Fe \citep{audi2017,krane1988}.

\emph{Absence of a repulsive strong force.}  By
Consequence~\ref{cons:chargeemergence}(b), nucleons sharing partition
structure within the nuclear envelope have no individual charge assignment;
the pairwise Coulomb-repulsion sum $\sum_{i<j}e^{2}/r_{ij}$ over interior
nucleon pairs is a sum over zero.  Only the total nuclear boundary charge
$+Ze$ enters observable electromagnetic processes.  Hence no ``strong force''
opposing a Coulomb repulsion interior to the nucleus is required; the
motivating problem that the strong force is invoked to solve does not arise
within this framework.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The Coulomb repulsion paradox dissolved]
\label{rem:coulombparadox}
The standard nuclear physics narrative posits that protons in a nucleus
experience enormous mutual Coulomb repulsion ($\sim$~few MeV per pair at
$\sim 1$~fm separation), and introduces a ``strong'' nuclear force to balance
this repulsion.  The strong force is then developed (pion exchange, meson
exchange, quark-level QCD) to account for both binding and saturation at
$\sim 8$~MeV/nucleon.

In the framework of this monograph, the motivation is absent.  The
``protons'' within a nucleus are not carriers of individual charge (by
Consequence~\ref{cons:chargeemergence}); only the boundary of the nuclear
partition envelope carries a definite charge $+Ze$.  The sum
$\sum_{i<j}e^{2}/r_{ij}$ is a sum over zeros.  The entire architecture of the
strong interaction as a solution to this problem is, in our framework,
unnecessary.  This is not a rejection of QCD's empirical success; it is a
reframing.  QCD and nuclear phenomenology describe the internal structure of
the nuclear partition boundary and the virtual-substate spectrum
(Consequence~\ref{cons:subnucleon})---both real and observable at
deep-inelastic resolution---not the balancing of a divergent Coulomb repulsion.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Saturation of nuclear binding]
\label{cor:saturation}
The per-nucleon binding energy $E_{\mathrm{bind}}/A$ approaches a constant
value $\approx 8$~MeV for $A\gtrsim 20$, independent of $A$ to within
$\sim 15\%$ deviation.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
From Consequence~\ref{cons:nuclearbinding},
$E_{\mathrm{bind}}/A = \kB T\ln b\cdot\Delta\Depth/A$.  The factor
$\Delta\Depth/A$ reflects the per-nucleon partition-depth reduction upon
joining the nucleus.  For nuclei large enough that the envelope is a closed
manifold, adding one more nucleon reduces the partition depth by a fixed
amount---the depth associated with one cell of the partition at nuclear
scale---so $\Delta\Depth/A\to\mathrm{const}$.  Deviations from the constant
arise from surface effects (nucleons on the envelope have reduced
coordination) and Coulomb corrections to the boundary charge; these are the
leading semi-empirical terms in the Bethe--Weizs\"acker formula
\citep{krane1988}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Alpha decay and beta decay}
\label{subsec:alphabeta}

The partition-boundary framework reproduces the empirical systematics of both
radioactive decay modes.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Alpha decay.}  A $^{4}$He nucleus is doubly-magic ($Z=2$, $N=2$,
  Consequence~\ref{cons:magicnumbers}) and therefore a maximally stable closed
  partition shell.  Alpha decay occurs when this closed-shell sub-structure can
  tunnel through the parent nucleus's boundary.  The tunnelling probability has
  the Gamow form $e^{-2G}$ with
  \[
    G = \int_{R}^{R'}\frac{\sqrt{2m(V-E)}}{\hbar}\,dr,
  \]
  derivable from the partition-depth gradient of the boundary.  The
  Geiger--Nuttall law (empirical correlation between decay constant and
  $Q$-value) follows from the $Q$-dependence of $G$.

\item \textbf{Beta decay.}  The weak-interaction partition couples an interior
  neutron to an interior proton plus virtual electron/antineutrino substates.
  The linearity of the Kurie plot follows from the phase-space density of
  allowed final states consistent with the partition coordinates.
\end{itemize}

Both decay systematics are consistent with the partition-boundary framework.
Detailed numerical derivation requires the specific structure of the
weak-interaction partition, which is not developed here.

\section{Magic Numbers}

\begin{consequence}{Nuclear Magic Numbers}{magicnumbers}
The shell capacity formula $C(n)=2n^{2}$ (Consequence~\ref{cons:shellcapacity})
applied to the nucleus with strong spin--orbit splitting yields the magic-number
sequence
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{M} = \{2,\,8,\,20,\,28,\,50,\,82,\,126\}.
  \label{eq:magicnumbers}
\end{equation}
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
The partition coordinates $(n,l,m,s)$ apply to nucleons within a nucleus
(Consequence~\ref{cons:nucleonosc}) with the same structure as the
electron coordinates in an atom.  Without spin--orbit coupling, the
cumulative shell capacity is $\sum_{n=1}^{n^{*}}C(n)=\sum_{n=1}^{n^{*}}2n^{2}$,
giving the harmonic-oscillator sequence $2,10,28,60,\ldots$

Adding the strong spin--orbit interaction present at the nuclear scale
(whose strength is $\ell\cdot s$-proportional and significantly larger than
the atomic fine-structure splitting) reorders the sub-shells: the
$j=l+\tfrac{1}{2}$ state is lowered relative to $j=l-\tfrac{1}{2}$ by a
splitting proportional to $l$.  This causes repeated crossings between
sub-shells of adjacent major shells.  Each crossing creates a new
closed-shell configuration.  Enumeration of the reordered filling sequence
\citep{mayer1949,haxeljensensuess1949,bohrmottelson1969,krane1988} yields
closed shells at exactly the magic numbers~\eqref{eq:magicnumbers}.

\begin{remark}
The magic numbers $\{2,8,20,28,50,82,126\}$ are a primary experimental
motivation for the nuclear shell model \citep{mayer1949,haxeljensensuess1949}.
Nuclei with a magic number of protons or neutrons (or both) are systematically
more stable, have higher natural abundances, and exhibit closed-shell features
(zero quadrupole moment, high first-excited-state energy).  All seven magic
numbers emerge here without free parameters beyond the identification of
$b=3$ and the existence of strong spin--orbit coupling at nuclear scale.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The isotope chart and the valley of stability}
\label{subsec:isotopechart}

The chart of all known stable and unstable nuclei (the ``valley of stability'')
shows a narrow band of stable configurations in the $(N,Z)$ plane centred
around $N\approx Z$ for light nuclei, drifting toward $N\approx 1.5Z$ for
heavy nuclei.  The drift is the boundary-charge Coulomb correction: more
protons require more neutrons to balance the nuclear envelope charge energy.

The Bethe--Weizs\"acker semi-empirical mass formula
\[
  E_{\mathrm{bind}}(A,Z)
  = a_{V}A - a_{S}A^{2/3} - a_{C}\frac{Z^{2}}{A^{1/3}}
    - a_{A}\frac{(A-2Z)^{2}}{A} + \delta(A,Z)
\]
fits the valley of stability quantitatively.  In the partition framework the
five terms correspond to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $a_{V}A$: volume (bulk) partition depth gain ($\Delta\Depth/A=$ const,
    Corollary~\ref{cor:saturation});
  \item $a_{S}A^{2/3}$: surface-nucleon coordination deficit (boundary cells
    have fewer neighbours);
  \item $a_{C}Z^{2}/A^{1/3}$: Coulomb energy of the boundary charge envelope
    (Consequence~\ref{cons:coulombenvelope});
  \item $a_{A}(A-2Z)^{2}/A$: asymmetry between neutron and proton partition
    cells (isospin asymmetry in the sub-coordinate structure);
  \item $\delta(A,Z)$: pairing term (closed sub-shell stability from
    Consequence~\ref{cons:shellcapacity}).
\end{itemize}
Deviations from the smooth Bethe--Weizs\"acker fit track magic numbers, closed
shells, and pairing effects---all Consequences of the partition structure.

\section{Emergence of Coulomb's Law}
\label{sec:coulombenvelope-nuclear}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{consequence}{Coulomb Envelope (Nuclear)}{coulombenvelope-nuclear}
The gradient of the partition depth field $\Depth(\mathbf{x})$ around a
localised boundary at the origin with $SO(3)$ symmetry and effective boundary
charge $+Ze$ has the asymptotic form, for $r\gg r_{\mathrm{boundary}}$,
\begin{equation}
  -\nabla\Depth(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{Ze^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r^{2}}\,\hat{\mathbf{r}},
  \label{eq:coulombenvelope-nuclear}
\end{equation}
reproducing Coulomb's law numerically.
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
By Consequence~\ref{cons:chargeemergence}, a localised boundary at the origin
with $SO(3)$ symmetry has its charge concentrated at the boundary surface.
The partition depth field $\Depth$ satisfies the same divergence-free condition
outside the boundary as the electric potential satisfies Laplace's equation in
empty space: Gauss's law applied to the partition depth field gives, for any
sphere of radius $r>r_{\mathrm{boundary}}$ enclosing the boundary,
\[
  4\pi r^{2}\,|\nabla\Depth| = \mathrm{const},
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  |\nabla\Depth| \propto \frac{1}{r^{2}}.
\]
The constant of proportionality is fixed by the boundary charge $+Ze$ and the
permittivity $\varepsilon_{0}$ relating partition-depth units to energy-per-charge
units.  Numerically, this is Coulomb's law as stated in~\eqref{eq:coulombenvelope-nuclear}.

No force postulate is invoked.  The gradient $-\nabla\Depth$ plays the role
of the electric field; the electron descends this gradient under the partition
equation of motion (Consequence~\ref{cons:poincare}), which is geometric
(gradient descent on a scalar field), not mediated by an external force
carrier.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[On the nature of the Coulomb force]
The conventional framework posits an electrostatic force
$F=Ze^{2}/(4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r^{2})$ acting between nucleus and electron,
mediated by the electromagnetic field and a virtual photon exchange.  In the
partition framework, no such force exists as a primitive.  The electron's
equation of motion is given by the gradient descent on $\Depth$ as determined
by the local partition geometry.  The $1/r^{2}$ gradient envelope emerges from
the divergence-free requirement on $\Depth$ in the region exterior to the
nuclear boundary combined with $SO(3)$ symmetry.  The appearance of this
envelope as ``Coulomb's law'' is exact in form but derivative in content: it
is not a separate physical law but a geometric Consequence of boundary
localisation and rotational symmetry.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Interior depth field is flat]
\label{prop:interiordepth-nuclear}
Inside the nuclear partition boundary $r<r_{\mathrm{boundary}}$, the partition
depth field $\Depth$ is approximately constant, so $-\nabla\Depth\approx 0$;
electrons placed inside the nucleus would experience no net gradient.
Consequence~\ref{cons:pauli} excludes atomic electron occupation of the
nuclear-volume cells, which are already occupied by nucleons.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Outside the nuclear boundary, Gauss's law applied to $-\nabla\Depth$ gives
$|-\nabla\Depth|\propto 1/r^{2}$ (Consequence~\ref{cons:coulombenvelope}).
Inside the boundary, the same theorem applied to a Gaussian surface entirely
within the boundary gives $|-\nabla\Depth|\propto r$ times the interior charge
density.  In the limit of a boundary-concentrated charge distribution, the
interior density is zero and therefore $-\nabla\Depth=0$ in the interior.
Consequence~\ref{cons:pauli} prevents atomic electrons from occupying the
nuclear cells, which are already filled by nucleons.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Coulomb envelope at the atomic scale}
\label{subsec:coulombatomic}

At distance $r$ from the nuclear centre, with $r\gg r_{\mathrm{boundary}}$
(nuclear radius $\sim 1$--$7$~fm, depending on $A$, and atomic radius
$\sim 10^{-10}$~m), the gradient field is
\[
  |-\nabla\Depth| = \frac{Ze^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r^{2}}.
\]
At $r\sim 10^{-10}$~m, this gives a force $eZ|\nabla\Depth|\sim 10^{-8}$~N
on an electron of charge $e$, producing typical electronic kinetic energies
of $\sim 10$~eV.  This matches observed ionisation energies of atoms.

\subsection{Inside the nucleus}
\label{subsec:insidenucleus}

For $r<r_{\mathrm{boundary}}$, the gradient field depends on the interior
charge distribution.  For a Fermi-distributed charge density (as measured in
heavy nuclei), the field rises approximately linearly from zero at the centre
to the Coulomb value at the surface, then falls as $1/r^{2}$ outside.  This
interior-rising-then-exterior-falling structure is observed in the nuclear
charge density of heavy nuclei \citep{hofstadter1956} and is consistent with
the partition-boundary interpretation: the charge is concentrated at the
boundary layer but has a smooth radial profile.
