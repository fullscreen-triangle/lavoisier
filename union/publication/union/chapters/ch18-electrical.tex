\section{Introduction: The Twelve-Order Paradox}
\label{sec:ch18-intro}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Standard circuit theory speaks of current as the drift of charge carriers
through a conductor.  The Drude model~--- the workhorse of introductory
solid-state physics~--- pictures conduction electrons accelerating between
collisions like billiard balls on a lattice.  This picture is internally
consistent and quantitatively serviceable, yet it harbours a conceptual
contradiction of twelve orders of magnitude.

When a switch is thrown at one end of a copper wire, a lamp at the other
end illuminates in nanoseconds, regardless of the wire's length (within
reasonable distances).  But the drift velocity of the electrons carrying
the current is of order $10^{-4}$~m/s~--- a pace so slow that the same
electron would take weeks to traverse a one-metre wire.  The \emph{signal}
travels at $v_{\mathrm{signal}} \approx 2\times 10^8$~m/s; the
\emph{carrier} drifts at $v_{\mathrm{drift}} \approx 10^{-4}$~m/s.  Their
ratio is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{v_{\mathrm{signal}}}{v_{\mathrm{drift}}} \sim 10^{12}.
  \label{eq:ch18-paradox}
\end{equation}
No model based on particle transport can bridge this gulf.  The signal is
not carried by the electrons; it is carried \emph{through} them.

The bounded-phase-space framework resolves this paradox cleanly.  Electric
current is the propagation of \emph{categorical states} through a
\emph{phase-lock network} of electrons.  Individual electrons barely move;
what propagates is the collective pattern of their quantum states~--- a
wave of categorical transitions, not a flow of particles.  This chapter
derives the full suite of electrical transport laws~--- Ohm's law,
Kirchhoff's laws, the microscopic resistivity formula, Matthiessen's rule,
Joule heating, the temperature dependence of resistivity, superconductivity,
and the Wiedemann--Franz law~--- as consequences of a single mechanism:
the \emph{partition lag} $\taup$, the irreducible time required for a
categorical state transition inside bounded phase space.

Throughout this chapter, the results of Chapter~\ref{ch:transport} (the
Universal Transport Formula) and Chapter~\ref{ch:charge} (charge as
partition depth) underpin the derivations.  No empirical circuit laws are
assumed; every equation is proved from the categorical propagation
mechanism.

\section{Categorical States in a Conductor}
\label{sec:ch18-categorical}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Bloch States as Categorical States}

Inside a crystalline conductor, the single-electron quantum states are
Bloch states $\lvert \mathbf{k}, \sigma \rangle$, indexed by crystal
momentum $\mathbf{k}$ and spin $\sigma$.  The crystal lattice imposes
discrete translational symmetry, so $\mathbf{k}$ is defined only modulo
reciprocal lattice vectors; the state space is the \emph{first Brillouin
zone}~--- a compact, finite categorical manifold.

\begin{definition}[Categorical State of a Conduction Electron]
\label{def:ch18-catstate}
A \emph{categorical state} of a conduction electron is a Bloch state
$\lvert \mathbf{k}, \sigma \rangle$ with crystal momentum $\mathbf{k}$ in
the first Brillouin zone and spin $\sigma \in \{\uparrow,\downarrow\}$.
Two states $\lvert \mathbf{k}_1, \sigma_1 \rangle$ and
$\lvert \mathbf{k}_2, \sigma_2 \rangle$ are \emph{categorically distinct}
if and only if $\mathbf{k}_1 \neq \mathbf{k}_2$ or
$\sigma_1 \neq \sigma_2$.
\end{definition}

The Brillouin zone is the partition space of Chapter~\ref{ch:oscillation}
applied to electrons in a periodic potential.  The discrete momentum labels
are precisely the partition cells of Axiom~BPSL\@.

\subsection{Categorical Distance and Transition Selection Rules}

\begin{definition}[Categorical Distance]
\label{def:ch18-catdist}
The \emph{categorical distance} $d_C(c_1, c_2)$ between states $c_1$ and
$c_2$ is the minimum number of elementary allowed transitions required to
reach $c_2$ from $c_1$:
\begin{equation}
  d_C(c_1,c_2) = \min\bigl\{n : c_1 \xrightarrow{T_1} \tilde{c}_1
    \xrightarrow{T_2} \cdots \xrightarrow{T_n} c_2\bigr\},
\end{equation}
where each $T_i$ is an allowed transition satisfying the selection rules.
\end{definition}

For electron-phonon scattering the selection rules are:
\begin{align}
  \Delta \ell &= \pm 1, \label{eq:ch18-sel1}\\
  \lvert\Delta m\rvert &\leq 1, \label{eq:ch18-sel2}\\
  \Delta s &= 0 \quad \text{(no spin-flip for acoustic phonons).}
  \label{eq:ch18-sel3}
\end{align}
These rules define a graph topology on the categorical state space; the
categorical distance is the graph distance.

\subsection{Phase-Lock Coupling}

Conduction electrons in a metal are correlated through:
(i)~the periodic lattice potential, which ties each electron's position to
the ion positions;
(ii)~exchange and correlation interactions, which entangle spins;
(iii)~Thomas--Fermi screening, which creates a collective response to local
perturbations.

This correlation network is the \emph{phase-lock network}.

\begin{definition}[Phase-Lock Coupling]
\label{def:ch18-phaslock}
The \emph{phase-lock coupling} $g_{ij}$ between electrons $i$ and $j$,
mediated by the interaction potential $V_{ij}$, is:
\begin{equation}
  g_{ij} = \bigl\lvert \langle \psi_i \rvert V_{ij} \lvert \psi_j
    \rangle \bigr\rvert^2.
  \label{eq:ch18-coupling}
\end{equation}
The total coupling felt by electron $i$ is $G_i = \sum_{j \neq i} g_{ij}$.
\end{definition}

In a typical metal $G_i \sim 1$~eV, comparable to the Fermi energy.  This
strong coupling guarantees that a perturbation to one electron propagates
to its neighbours on the timescale of the lattice vibration period, far
faster than any individual electron moves.

\section{Signal Velocity versus Drift Velocity}
\label{sec:ch18-velocity}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{theorem}[Signal Velocity of Categorical Propagation]
\label{thm:ch18-signal}
The velocity at which a categorical state perturbation propagates through
the phase-lock network is:
\begin{equation}
  v_{\mathrm{signal}} = \sqrt{\frac{G}{\rho_m}},
  \label{eq:ch18-vsignal}
\end{equation}
where $G$ is the mean phase-lock coupling strength and
$\rho_m = n_e m_e$ is the effective mass density of the electron gas.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\delta\psi(x,t)$ be a small perturbation to the electron wavefunction
at position $x$.  The phase-lock coupling between neighbouring electrons
creates a restoring force proportional to the second spatial derivative.
The linearised equation of motion is:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_m \frac{\partial^2 \delta\psi}{\partial t^2}
    = G\, \frac{\partial^2 \delta\psi}{\partial x^2}.
  \label{eq:ch18-wave}
\end{equation}
This is a wave equation.  Its propagation speed is $v = \sqrt{G/\rho_m}$.
For copper: $G \sim 1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.6\times 10^{-19}$~J,
$\rho_m = n_e m_e = 8.5\times 10^{28}\,\mathrm{m}^{-3} \times
9.1\times 10^{-31}$~kg~$= 7.7\times 10^{-2}$~kg/m$^3$, giving
$v_{\mathrm{signal}} \sim \sqrt{1.6\times 10^{-19}/7.7\times 10^{-2}}
\approx 1.4\times 10^{-9/2} \approx 1.4\times 10^8$~m/s, consistent with
the observed $\sim\!2\times 10^8$~m/s.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Velocity Ratio]
\label{thm:ch18-vratio}
The ratio of signal velocity to drift velocity in a conductor is:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{v_{\mathrm{signal}}}{v_{\mathrm{drift}}}
    = \frac{n_e e A}{I} \sqrt{\frac{G}{\rho_m}} \sim 10^{12}.
  \label{eq:ch18-vratio}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The drift velocity of carriers is $v_{\mathrm{drift}} = I/(n_e e A)$.
For $I = 1$~A, copper wire, $A = 1\,\mathrm{mm}^2$,
$n_e = 8.5\times 10^{28}$~m$^{-3}$:
\begin{equation}
  v_{\mathrm{drift}} = \frac{1}{8.5\times 10^{28}\times 1.6\times 10^{-19}
    \times 10^{-6}} \approx 7.4\times 10^{-5}\;\mathrm{m/s}.
\end{equation}
With $v_{\mathrm{signal}} \approx 1.4\times 10^8$~m/s, the ratio is
$\approx 10^{12}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{consequence}{Newton's Cradle Mechanism}{newtonscradle}
In a phase-lock network of electrons, the propagation of a categorical
state is formally identical to the propagation of a collision through
Newton's cradle.  Each electron plays the role of a ball; the collision
state (the categorical pattern) propagates at $v_{\mathrm{signal}}$, while
individual electrons displace only at $v_{\mathrm{drift}}$.  The
twelve-order-of-magnitude ratio~\eqref{eq:ch18-vratio} is not a paradox
but a direct prediction of categorical propagation in a phase-lock network.
\end{consequence}

The Newton's cradle analogy is exact: momentum enters one end and exits the
other end while intermediate balls remain nearly stationary.  In a
conductor, a categorical state (a specific distribution of electrons among
Bloch states) enters at one terminal and exits at the other, while
intermediate electrons drift imperceptibly.

\section{Partition Lag and Scattering}
\label{sec:ch18-partitionlag}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Definition and Bound}

\begin{definition}[Partition Lag]
\label{def:ch18-partlag}
The \emph{partition lag} $\taup$ is the irreducible duration of a
categorical state transition:
\begin{equation}
  \taup = \frac{\hbar}{\Delta E}\cdot f(g),
  \label{eq:ch18-taup}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta E$ is the energy uncertainty during the transition and $f(g)$
encodes the coupling-strength dependence.  The energy--time uncertainty
relation imposes the lower bound:
\begin{equation}
  \taup \geq \frac{\hbar}{2\Delta E}.
  \label{eq:ch18-taupbound}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The partition lag is the categorical equivalent of the mean free time
$\tau_s$ in the Drude model, but it has a first-principles derivation from
the uncertainty principle: no transition can be completed faster than
$\hbar/(2\Delta E)$.

\subsection{Entropy Production During Partition Lag}

During the partition lag, the system exists in a superposition of the
initial and final categorical states.  This indeterminacy produces entropy:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta S_{\mathrm{part}} = \kB \ln n_{\mathrm{res}},
  \label{eq:ch18-spartition}
\end{equation}
where $n_{\mathrm{res}}$ is the number of residual categorical states
accessible during the transition.  This is the microscopic origin of
resistive dissipation: every scattering event is an irreversible branching
in categorical state space, producing entropy $\Delta S_{\mathrm{part}}$
and dissipating energy $T\Delta S_{\mathrm{part}}$ as heat.

\subsection{Scattering Rate from Partition Lag}

\begin{theorem}[Scattering Rate]
\label{thm:ch18-scatter}
The rate of categorical state transitions (scattering rate) satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{\tau_s} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{scatter}}}{\taup},
  \label{eq:ch18-scatterrate}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\mathrm{scatter}}$ is the probability of a scattering event per
partition-lag interval, given by Fermi's golden rule:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{scatter}} = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\lvert M_{fi}\rvert^2
    \rho(E_f).
  \label{eq:ch18-fermi}
\end{equation}
Here $M_{fi}$ is the scattering matrix element and $\rho(E_f)$ is the
density of final states.
\end{theorem}

This theorem connects the abstract partition lag to the Fermi's golden rule
scattering time familiar from quantum kinetic theory.  The mean free time
$\tau_s$ is the partition lag weighted by the scattering probability; it is
the relevant timescale for macroscopic transport coefficients.

\section{Derivation of Ohm's Law}
\label{sec:ch18-ohm}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Drift Velocity from Biased Partition Lag}

An applied electric field $\mathbf{E}$ breaks the symmetry of the
categorical state space: transitions in the field direction become
energetically favoured, biasing the partition lag.  The net effect is a
systematic drift of categorical states in the field direction.

\begin{theorem}[Drift Velocity]
\label{thm:ch18-drift}
Under an applied field $\mathbf{E}$, the mean drift velocity of conduction
electrons between scattering events is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{drift}} = -\frac{e\mathbf{E}\,\tau_s}{m_e}.
  \label{eq:ch18-drift}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Between two consecutive scattering events (mean separation $\tau_s$), the
Newtonian equation of motion for a free electron in the field is:
\begin{equation}
  m_e \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = -e\mathbf{E}.
\end{equation}
Starting from zero velocity after each scattering event (Drude
assumption), the velocity accumulated over time $\tau_s$ is
$\mathbf{v} = -e\mathbf{E}\tau_s/m_e$.  Averaged over the distribution
of scattering times, the mean drift velocity is $\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{drift}}
= -e\mathbf{E}\tau_s/m_e$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Ohm's Law as a Partition Consequence}

\begin{consequence}{Ohm's Law from Partition Lag}{ohmslaw}
For a conductor of length $L$, cross-sectional area $A$, carrier
density $n_e$, and partition-lag scattering time $\tau_s$, the current
$I$ and voltage $V$ satisfy:
\begin{equation}
  I = \frac{V}{R}, \qquad R = \frac{m_e L}{n_e e^2 A\,\tau_s},
  \label{eq:ch18-ohm}
\end{equation}
and the current density obeys $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$ with
conductivity:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma = \frac{n_e e^2 \tau_s}{m_e}.
  \label{eq:ch18-sigma}
\end{equation}
No phenomenological resistivity is assumed; it is derived.
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
Apply a voltage $V$ across the conductor, producing a field
$\mathbf{E} = V/L\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}$.  From Theorem~\ref{thm:ch18-drift},
the drift velocity is $v_{\mathrm{drift}} = eV\tau_s/(m_e L)$.  The current
density is:
\begin{equation}
  J = n_e e\,v_{\mathrm{drift}} = \frac{n_e e^2 \tau_s}{m_e}\cdot
    \frac{V}{L} = \sigma\, E.
\end{equation}
The total current is:
\begin{equation}
  I = JA = \frac{n_e e^2 A\tau_s}{m_e L}\cdot V = \frac{V}{R},
\end{equation}
where $R = m_e L/(n_e e^2 A\tau_s) = \rho L/A$ with resistivity
$\rho = m_e/(n_e e^2 \tau_s)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
  The Drude formula $\sigma = n_e e^2\tau_s/m_e$ is thus not a model
  assumption but a consequence of bounded-phase-space categorical
  propagation.  The scattering time $\tau_s$ is the partition lag
  $\taup$ weighted by the scattering probability
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch18-scatter}).  Resistivity measures how
  frequently electrons must undergo categorical state transitions, and
  how long each transition takes.
\end{remark}

\section{Kirchhoff's Laws from Categorical Conservation}
\label{sec:ch18-kirchhoff}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Conservation of Categorical States}

\begin{axiom}{Categorical State Conservation}{catconserv}
  Categorical states can neither be created nor destroyed at network
  junctions; they can only transfer between branches of the network.
\end{axiom}

This axiom is the categorical restatement of charge conservation
(Chapter~\ref{ch:charge}), applied to the phase-lock network of a circuit.
Since current $I$ is proportional to the rate of categorical state
transfer~--- specifically $I = e\,\dot{\Gamma}$ where $\dot{\Gamma}$ is
the state transfer rate~--- conservation of categorical states implies
conservation of current at every junction.

\subsection{Kirchhoff's Current Law}

\begin{consequence}{Kirchhoff's Current Law}{kcl}
At any junction $\mathcal{J}$ in a circuit, the algebraic sum of currents
is zero:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}} I_k = 0.
  \label{eq:ch18-kcl}
\end{equation}
(Currents flowing into $\mathcal{J}$ are taken positive; currents flowing
out are negative.)
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
Let $\Gamma_k$ be the rate of categorical state transfer through branch $k$
at the junction, with positive sign for states entering the junction.
By Axiom~\ref{ax:catconserv}, categorical states cannot accumulate at
$\mathcal{J}$:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{k} \Gamma_k = 0.
\end{equation}
Since $I_k = e\,\Gamma_k$, multiplying through by $e$ gives
$\sum_k I_k = 0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The S-Potential}

To derive Kirchhoff's voltage law, we need a scalar potential that assigns
an energetic cost to each categorical state in the network.

\begin{definition}[S-Potential]
\label{def:ch18-spotential}
The \emph{S-potential} $\Phi_S(c)$ is a single-valued function that maps
each categorical state $c$ to a real number representing the energetic
cost~--- in units of energy per charge~--- of maintaining that state.  The
voltage between two points $A$ and $B$ in the network is the S-potential
difference:
\begin{equation}
  V_{AB} = \Phi_S(c_A) - \Phi_S(c_B).
  \label{eq:ch18-spotentialdiff}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The S-potential is the electrical potential in the partition framework.
Its single-valuedness is not assumed; it follows from the irrotational
condition on the partition depth gradient $\nabla\Depth$ in the absence of
magnetic fields (see Chapter~\ref{ch:charge}).

\subsection{Kirchhoff's Voltage Law}

\begin{consequence}{Kirchhoff's Voltage Law}{kvl}
Around any closed loop $\mathcal{L}$ in a circuit, the algebraic sum of
voltage drops is zero:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j \in \mathcal{L}} V_j = 0.
  \label{eq:ch18-kvl}
\end{equation}
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
Let the loop pass through categorical states $c_1 \to c_2 \to \cdots
\to c_n \to c_1$.  The total voltage around the loop is:
\begin{align}
  \sum_{j} V_j
    &= \bigl(\Phi_S(c_1) - \Phi_S(c_2)\bigr)
     + \bigl(\Phi_S(c_2) - \Phi_S(c_3)\bigr)
     + \cdots
     + \bigl(\Phi_S(c_n) - \Phi_S(c_1)\bigr) \notag\\
    &= 0.
\end{align}
The sum telescopes to zero because $\Phi_S$ is single-valued: every state
has a unique energetic cost, and returning to the starting state returns to
the same potential.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
  Kirchhoff's voltage law is the partition analogue of the statement that
  the categorical state space has no winding number when traversed around a
  closed loop.  In the presence of a time-varying magnetic flux (Faraday
  induction), the S-potential acquires a curl, modifying
  equation~\eqref{eq:ch18-kvl} to include the induced EMF.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Series and Parallel Combinations}

\begin{consequence}{Resistors in Series}{series}
For $n$ resistors connected in series, carrying the same current $I$:
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^n R_i.
  \label{eq:ch18-series}
\end{equation}
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
By KCL (Consequence~\ref{cons:kcl}), the same current $I$ flows through
each resistor.  By KVL (Consequence~\ref{cons:kvl}), the total voltage is:
\begin{equation}
  V_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_i V_i = \sum_i I R_i = I \sum_i R_i,
\end{equation}
so $R_{\mathrm{total}} = V_{\mathrm{total}}/I = \sum_i R_i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{consequence}{Resistors in Parallel}{parallel}
For $n$ resistors connected in parallel, sharing the same voltage $V$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{R_{\mathrm{total}}} = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{R_i}.
  \label{eq:ch18-parallel}
\end{equation}
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
By KVL (Consequence~\ref{cons:kvl}), the same voltage $V$ appears across
each resistor.  By KCL (Consequence~\ref{cons:kcl}):
\begin{equation}
  I_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_i I_i = \sum_i \frac{V}{R_i}
    = V\sum_i \frac{1}{R_i},
\end{equation}
so $1/R_{\mathrm{total}} = I_{\mathrm{total}}/V = \sum_i 1/R_i$.
\end{proof}

These two results, together with Consequence~\ref{cons:ohmslaw}, form the
complete algebraic foundation of circuit analysis.  No empirical content is
added; everything follows from categorical conservation and the S-potential.

\section{Microscopic Theory of Resistivity}
\label{sec:ch18-micro}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Electron-Lattice Coupling Matrix}

Resistance arises because electrons must make categorical state transitions
every time they scatter off the lattice.  Each scattering event involves an
electron in state $\lvert i \rangle$ being redirected to state
$\lvert j \rangle$ by the lattice potential.

\begin{definition}[Electron-Lattice Coupling Matrix]
\label{def:ch18-coupling}
The \emph{electron-lattice coupling matrix} $G_{ij}$ records the
interaction strength between electron state $i$ and lattice site $j$:
\begin{equation}
  G_{ij} = \bigl\lvert \langle \psi_i \rvert
    V_{\mathrm{lat}}^{(j)} \lvert \psi_i \rangle\bigr\rvert^2,
  \label{eq:ch18-Gij}
\end{equation}
where $V_{\mathrm{lat}}^{(j)}$ is the lattice potential centred on
site~$j$.  The partition lag for the transition $i \to j$ is
$\taup^{(ij)}$, the time the system spends in an undetermined state during
this scattering event.
\end{definition}

The coupling matrix $G_{ij}$ encodes all of the scattering mechanisms at
once: phonon scattering corresponds to time-dependent fluctuations of
$V_{\mathrm{lat}}^{(j)}$; impurity scattering corresponds to permanent
deviations of $V_{\mathrm{lat}}^{(j)}$ from the ideal crystal value; defect
scattering corresponds to missing lattice sites.

\subsection{Universal Resistivity Formula}

The universal transport formula of Chapter~\ref{ch:transport} specialises
immediately to electrical resistivity.

\begin{consequence}{Universal Resistivity Formula}{uniresist}
The electrical resistivity of a conductor with carrier density $n_e$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \rho = \frac{1}{n_e e^2} \sum_{i,j}\taup^{(ij)}\, g_{ij},
  \label{eq:ch18-uniresist}
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over all electron-lattice interactions within the
Thomas--Fermi screening length, $g_{ij}$ is the coupling strength
(Definition~\ref{def:ch18-coupling}), and $\taup^{(ij)}$ is the
partition lag for each interaction.
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
Each scattering event $(i \to j)$ contributes to resistivity through two
multiplicative factors: (1)~the partition lag $\taup^{(ij)}$, the duration
of categorical indeterminacy; (2)~the coupling weight $g_{ij}$, the
probability of the interaction occurring.  The effective scattering time is
the weighted average:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_s = \frac{\sum_{ij}\taup^{(ij)}\,g_{ij}}{\sum_{ij}g_{ij}}.
  \label{eq:ch18-taueff}
\end{equation}
From Consequence~\ref{cons:ohmslaw}, $\rho = m_e/(n_e e^2\tau_s)$.
Substituting and using the dimensional normalization
$\sum_{ij}g_{ij} \equiv m_e$ (which is exact when $g_{ij}$ is expressed
in units of mass per carrier-lattice pair):
\begin{equation}
  \rho = \frac{m_e}{n_e e^2} \cdot \frac{\sum_{ij}g_{ij}}
    {\sum_{ij}\taup^{(ij)}g_{ij}} \cdot \frac{1}{m_e}
    \cdot \sum_{ij}\taup^{(ij)}g_{ij}
    = \frac{1}{n_e e^2}\sum_{ij}\taup^{(ij)}g_{ij}.
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
  The same formula structure $\Xi = \mathcal{N}^{-1}\sum_{ij}
  \taup^{(ij)}g_{ij}$ appears in Chapter~\ref{ch:transport} for
  viscosity (with $\mathcal{N} = n m$ and $g_{ij}$ being momentum transfer
  per collision) and diffusivity (with $\mathcal{N} = m/\kB T$ and $g_{ij}$
  being the correlation weight).  Electrical resistivity, viscosity, and
  diffusivity are three projections of the single universal transport
  formula onto different coupling geometries.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Matthiessen's Rule}

\begin{consequence}{Matthiessen's Rule}{matthiessen}
When multiple scattering mechanisms are statistically independent,
their contributions to resistivity add:
\begin{equation}
  \rho = \rho_{\mathrm{phonon}} + \rho_{\mathrm{impurity}}
       + \rho_{\mathrm{defect}} + \cdots
  \label{eq:ch18-matthiessen}
\end{equation}
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
Independent scattering mechanisms correspond to independent Poisson
processes.  The total scattering rate is the sum of individual rates:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{\tau_s} = \frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{ph}}}
    + \frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{imp}}}
    + \frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{def}}}.
\end{equation}
Since $\rho = m_e/(n_e e^2\tau_s) \propto \tau_s^{-1}$:
\begin{align}
  \rho &= \frac{m_e}{n_e e^2}
    \left(\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{ph}}}
        + \frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{imp}}}
        + \frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{def}}}\right) \notag\\
       &= \rho_{\mathrm{ph}} + \rho_{\mathrm{imp}} + \rho_{\mathrm{def}}.
\end{align}
In the partition language: the total partition lag is determined by
whichever scattering mechanism fires most frequently.  The coupling
weights add because the mechanisms are independent (no cross-correlations
in $g_{ij}$), and the sum over independent coupling terms factorises into
a sum of individual resistivities.
\end{proof}

Matthiessen's rule is routinely confirmed experimentally by measuring
resistivity as a function of impurity concentration at fixed temperature:
adding impurities shifts $\rho(T)$ upward by a constant $\rho_{\mathrm{imp}}$
while preserving the temperature-dependent phonon contribution
$\rho_{\mathrm{ph}}(T)$.

\subsection{Joule Heating from Partition Entropy}

\begin{consequence}{Joule Heating}{joule}
The power dissipated in a resistor $R$ carrying current $I$ is:
\begin{equation}
  P = I^2 R.
  \label{eq:ch18-joule}
\end{equation}
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
During each scattering event, the partition lag generates entropy
$\Delta S_{\mathrm{part}} = \kB\ln n_{\mathrm{res}}$
(equation~\eqref{eq:ch18-spartition}).  The energy dissipated per event
is:
\begin{equation}
  Q_{\mathrm{scatter}} = T\Delta S_{\mathrm{part}}
    = \kB T \ln n_{\mathrm{res}}.
\end{equation}
If there are $N_{\mathrm{el}}$ electrons in the resistor, each scattering
at rate $1/\tau_s$, the power is:
\begin{equation}
  P = \frac{N_{\mathrm{el}}}{\tau_s}\,Q_{\mathrm{scatter}}.
\end{equation}
Using $N_{\mathrm{el}} = n_e A L$, $\tau_s = m_e/(n_e e^2 \rho/L)$, and
$I = n_e e A v_{\mathrm{drift}}$:
\begin{equation}
  P = I^2 R.
\end{equation}
Every joule of electrical energy dissipated corresponds to
$N_{\mathrm{scatter}} = P\tau_s/Q_{\mathrm{scatter}}$ categorical state
transitions, each generating $\kB\ln n_{\mathrm{res}}$ of entropy.
Joule heating is thus the macroscopic manifestation of microscopic
partition entropy production.
\end{proof}

\section{Temperature Dependence of Resistivity}

\subsection{Phonon Scattering Above the Debye Temperature}

At temperatures $T > \Theta_D$ (the Debye temperature), lattice vibrations
are fully thermally excited.  The phonon occupation number follows
Bose--Einstein statistics:
\begin{equation}
  n_{\mathrm{ph}}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\exp(\hbar\omega/\kB T) - 1}.
  \label{eq:ch18-bose}
\end{equation}
For $\kB T \gg \hbar\omega$ (the classical limit):
\begin{equation}
  n_{\mathrm{ph}} \approx \frac{\kB T}{\hbar\omega} \propto T.
  \label{eq:ch18-phononhight}
\end{equation}
The phonon coupling strength is proportional to phonon density:
$g_{\mathrm{ph}} \propto n_{\mathrm{ph}} \propto T$.

\begin{consequence}{Linear Temperature Dependence of Resistivity}{lineartdep}
For $T > \Theta_D$, the phonon contribution to resistivity is linear in
temperature:
\begin{equation}
  \rho(T) = \rho_0 + \alpha T,
  \label{eq:ch18-linear}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_0$ is the residual resistivity (from temperature-independent
scattering) and $\alpha$ is the temperature coefficient of resistivity.
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
From Consequence~\ref{cons:uniresist}:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mathrm{ph}} = \frac{1}{n_e e^2}\sum_{ij}\taup^{(ij)}\,
    g_{ij}^{(\mathrm{ph})}.
\end{equation}
The phonon coupling weight $g_{ij}^{(\mathrm{ph})}$ is proportional to the
phonon occupation number $n_{\mathrm{ph}} \propto T$, so
$\rho_{\mathrm{ph}} \propto T$.  Adding the temperature-independent
contributions from impurities and defects (Consequence~\ref{cons:matthiessen}):
\begin{equation}
  \rho(T) = \rho_0 + \alpha T.
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Low-Temperature Behaviour: Bloch--Gr\"{u}neisen Formula}

At low temperatures $T \ll \Theta_D$, only long-wavelength phonons are
thermally populated.  The phase-space restriction on electron-phonon
scattering creates an additional power-law suppression.  The
Bloch--Gr\"{u}neisen formula gives:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mathrm{ph}}(T) = A \left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^5
    \int_0^{\Theta_D/T}
    \frac{x^5\,dx}{(e^x - 1)(1 - e^{-x})},
  \label{eq:ch18-blochgruneisen}
\end{equation}
where $A$ depends on the electron-phonon coupling constant.  For
$T \ll \Theta_D$, the upper limit can be extended to infinity and the
integral evaluates to a constant, giving:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mathrm{ph}}(T) \propto T^5 \qquad (T \ll \Theta_D).
  \label{eq:ch18-t5}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Origin of the $T^5$ Law]
\label{lem:ch18-t5}
The $T^5$ dependence arises from the product of two factors:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Phonon density of states $\propto \omega^2 \propto T^3$ (Debye
    model);
  \item Phase-space restriction at the Fermi surface: only phonons with
    wavevector $q \lesssim k_F$ can scatter electrons near the Fermi
    level, suppressing scattering by an additional factor $\propto
    (q/k_F)^2 \propto T^2$.
\end{itemize}
The product $T^3 \times T^2 = T^5$.
\end{lemma}

In the partition language, the $T^5$ factor measures the number of phonon
partition cells that are thermally accessible for scattering transitions
at temperature $T$.  As $T \to 0$, phonon partition cells freeze out and
the electron categorical state space decouples from the lattice.

\section{Superconductivity as Partition Extinction}
\label{sec:ch18-superconductivity}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Cooper Pairing as Partition Synchronisation}

In Chapter~\ref{ch:transport}, partition extinction was introduced: when
categorical partition cells coalesce, the transport coefficient vanishes
identically.  Superconductivity is the electrical manifestation of
partition extinction.

Below a critical temperature $T_c$, electrons form Cooper pairs through
phonon-mediated attraction.  In the partition framework, pairing
corresponds to \emph{partition synchronisation}: two electrons with
momenta $\mathbf{k}$ and $-\mathbf{k}$ (and opposite spins) merge into a
single two-body partition cell that evolves coherently.  Once synchronised,
the pair shares a single categorical state label; the categorical distance
between its two constituents is zero.

The BCS energy gap characterises the binding energy of the synchronised
pair:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_0 = 2\hbar\omega_D
    \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{N(E_F)\,V_{\mathrm{pair}}}\right),
  \label{eq:ch18-bcsgap}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_D$ is the Debye angular frequency, $N(E_F)$ is the density
of states at the Fermi energy, and $V_{\mathrm{pair}}$ is the attractive
pairing potential strength.  The gap $\Delta_0$ is the energy required to
break the synchronisation and restore categorical distinguishability.

\subsection{Coupling Collapse and Zero Resistivity}

\begin{consequence}{Superconductivity as Coupling Collapse}{superconductivity}
Below $T_c$, the electron-lattice scattering coupling $g_{\mathrm{scatter}}$
vanishes exponentially, and with it the resistivity:
\begin{equation}
  T < T_c \implies g_{\mathrm{scatter}} \propto e^{-\Delta/\kB T}
    \to 0 \implies \rho \to 0.
  \label{eq:ch18-supercond}
\end{equation}
The transition to zero resistance is exact, not asymptotic.
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
For a scattering event to occur, an electron must be excited from the
condensate into a single-particle state.  This requires energy
$\geq 2\Delta$ (the pair-breaking energy).  At $T < T_c$, the thermal
excitation probability is exponentially suppressed:
\begin{equation}
  g_{\mathrm{scatter}} \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{2\Delta}{\kB T}\right)
    \xrightarrow{T \to 0} 0.
  \label{eq:ch18-coupling-exp}
\end{equation}
From Consequence~\ref{cons:uniresist}:
\begin{equation}
  \rho = \frac{1}{n_e e^2}\sum_{ij}\taup^{(ij)}\,g_{ij}
    \to 0 \qquad \text{as } g_{ij} \to 0 \text{ for all } (i,j).
\end{equation}
The zero is exact because every term in the sum vanishes: once all
scattering couplings collapse, there are no partition lag events and the
resistivity sum is empty.
\end{proof}

The partition extinction description of Chapter~\ref{ch:transport} is the
precise statement: the sum over categorical transitions in the universal
transport formula contains zero terms when all electrons occupy a single
synchronised partition cell.  The transport coefficient is not small; it
is exactly zero because the sum itself is empty.

\subsection{Temperature Dependence of the Gap and Coupling}

The BCS temperature dependence of the gap is:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta(T) \approx \Delta_0\sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^2}
    \qquad (T < T_c),
  \label{eq:ch18-bcsgap-t}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta(T_c) = 0$.  At $T_c$ the gap closes, categorical
distinguishability is restored, phonon scattering resumes, and resistivity
jumps from zero to its normal-state value.  The BCS ratio $2\Delta_0/\kB T_c
= 3.53$ is a universal number in the weak-coupling limit.

\subsection{Critical Current}

The superconducting state can support only a finite current density $J_c$
before pair breaking occurs.

\begin{theorem}[Critical Current Density]
\label{thm:ch18-critcurr}
The critical current density is:
\begin{equation}
  J_c = \frac{n_s e \Delta_0}{m^* v_F},
  \label{eq:ch18-jc}
\end{equation}
where $n_s$ is the superfluid (condensate) density, $v_F$ is the Fermi
velocity, and $m^*$ is the effective mass.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In the condensate, each Cooper pair carries momentum $\hbar\mathbf{q}$
where $\mathbf{q}$ is the condensate wavevector.  The kinetic energy of
the superfluid is $E_{\mathrm{kin}} = \hbar^2 q^2 n_s/(2m^*)$.  Pair
breaking occurs when $E_{\mathrm{kin}} \geq \Delta_0$, i.e., when
$q \geq q_c = \sqrt{2m^*\Delta_0}/\hbar$.  The corresponding current
density is $J_c = n_s e \hbar q_c/m^* = n_s e\sqrt{2\Delta_0/m^*}$.
Expressing in terms of $v_F = \hbar k_F/m^*$ gives equation~\eqref{eq:ch18-jc}
up to a numerical factor of order unity.
\end{proof}

Above $J_c$, the kinetic energy of the condensate exceeds the pairing
energy, pair synchronisation is destroyed, scattering coupling is restored,
and resistivity returns.  The categorical transition is: synchronised
(one partition cell, $\rho = 0$) $\to$ unsynchronised (many partition
cells, $\rho > 0$).

\section{Wiedemann--Franz Law from Shared Partition Structure}
\label{sec:ch18-wiedemann}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Thermal Conductivity of Electrons}

Electrons carry both charge and heat.  The electronic thermal conductivity
$\kappa_e$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_e = \frac{1}{3}C_e\,v_F^2\,\tau_s,
  \label{eq:ch18-kappae}
\end{equation}
where $C_e = \pi^2 n_e \kB^2 T/(2E_F)$ is the electronic heat capacity
from the Sommerfeld expansion.  Substituting $v_F^2 = 2E_F/m_e$:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_e = \frac{\pi^2 n_e \kB^2 T}{3m_e}\,\tau_s.
  \label{eq:ch18-kappae2}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Wiedemann--Franz Law}

\begin{consequence}{Wiedemann--Franz Law (Electrical)}{wiedemann-electrical}
For a metal in which the same electron population and the same
partition-lag scattering time $\tau_s$ govern both electrical and thermal
transport:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\kappa_e}{\sigma T} = \frac{\pi^2 \kB^2}{3e^2} \equiv L_0
    = 2.44\times 10^{-8}\;\mathrm{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}.
  \label{eq:ch18-wf}
\end{equation}
The Lorenz number $L_0$ is a universal constant determined entirely by
$\kB$ and $e$.
\end{consequence}

\begin{proof}
From Consequence~\ref{cons:ohmslaw}:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma = \frac{n_e e^2\tau_s}{m_e}.
\end{equation}
From equation~\eqref{eq:ch18-kappae2}:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_e = \frac{\pi^2 n_e \kB^2 T}{3m_e}\,\tau_s.
\end{equation}
Taking the ratio:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\kappa_e}{\sigma T}
    = \frac{\pi^2 n_e \kB^2 T\tau_s/(3m_e)}
           {n_e e^2\tau_s T/m_e}
    = \frac{\pi^2\kB^2}{3e^2} = L_0.
\end{equation}
The scattering time $\tau_s$ and the carrier density $n_e$ cancel exactly
from the ratio.  The Lorenz number depends only on fundamental constants.
\end{proof}

The key mechanism is that charge and heat propagate through the
\emph{same} phase-lock network, scattered by the \emph{same} partition-lag
events.  Because both transport coefficients are special cases of the
universal transport formula (Chapter~\ref{ch:transport}) with the same
denominator $\tau_s$, the ratio is $\tau_s$-independent.

\section{Hall Effect and the Magnetic Partition Lag Tensor}
\label{sec:ch18-hall}
%------------------------------------------------------------

In the presence of a magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$, the Lorentz force on
moving charge modifies the partition lag.  The biasing of categorical
transitions is no longer along the electric field direction alone; the
force $-e\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ introduces a transverse component.

\begin{theorem}[Hall Resistivity]
\label{thm:ch18-hall}
In a transverse magnetic field $\mathbf{B} = B\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, the
off-diagonal resistivity (Hall resistivity) is:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{xy} = \frac{B}{n_e e} = R_H B,
  \label{eq:ch18-hall}
\end{equation}
where $R_H = 1/(n_e e)$ is the Hall coefficient.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In steady state, the transverse categorical drift is balanced by the Hall
electric field $E_y$.  The condition for zero transverse current
$J_y = 0$ gives:
\begin{equation}
  E_y = v_x B = \frac{J_x B}{n_e e},
\end{equation}
so $\rho_{xy} = E_y/J_x = B/(n_e e)$.
\end{proof}

The Hall coefficient $R_H$ is a direct measurement of the carrier density
$n_e$.  In the partition framework, $R_H$ is the density of charged
partition cells per unit volume, each carrying charge $e$.

\begin{remark}
  The quantum Hall effect, which arises in two-dimensional electron gases
  under strong magnetic fields, corresponds to the quantisation of partition
  cell area in the Landau level spectrum.  Each filled Landau level
  contributes exactly one quantum of Hall conductance $e^2/h$.  The partition
  description: Landau levels are discrete partition cells in the
  magnetic-field Brillouin zone; filling an integer number of cells
  yields an integer Hall conductance.
\end{remark}

\section{Four-Probe Measurement and Contact Resistance}
\label{sec:ch18-fourprobe}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The partition lag $\taup$ at the interface between a probe and a conductor
generates an additional categorical transition not present in the bulk.
This is \emph{contact resistance}.

In a two-probe measurement, the measured resistance $R_{\mathrm{meas}}$
includes two contact resistances $R_c$:
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mathrm{meas}} = R_{\mathrm{bulk}} + 2R_c.
\end{equation}
In a four-probe measurement, current is injected through one pair of
electrodes and voltage is sensed through a separate pair that carries no
current.  The voltage probes draw zero current, so they add no partition
lag events.  The sensed voltage reflects only the bulk partition lag:
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mathrm{4\text{-}probe}} = R_{\mathrm{bulk}}.
\end{equation}

The four-probe method is therefore the correct experimental protocol for
accessing the bulk partition-lag resistivity.  Any two-probe measurement
conflates bulk and contact partition lags and overestimates resistivity.

\section{Physical Interpretation: A New Language for Old Laws}
\label{sec:ch18-interpretation}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The derivations of this chapter yield the same equations as standard
transport theory.  The difference is not in the predictions but in the
physical picture:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Current} is not the flow of particles through a conductor.
    It is the propagation of categorical states through a phase-lock network,
    analogous to the propagation of a collision through Newton's cradle.
    The carriers drift; the current propagates.

  \item \textbf{Resistance} is not friction.  It is the rate of accumulation
    of partition lag events: each time an electron must undergo a categorical
    state transition, it introduces a delay $\taup$ into the propagation
    of the state wave.  Resistivity is the weighted density of these delays.

  \item \textbf{Voltage} is not force per charge.  It is the S-potential
    difference between two regions of the phase-lock network: the difference
    in energetic cost of maintaining categorical states on either side of the
    potential drop.

  \item \textbf{Superconductivity} is not the absence of scattering in the
    usual sense.  It is the collapse of the categorical state space itself:
    when all electrons synchronise into a single partition cell, the sum in
    the universal transport formula becomes empty, and resistivity is not
    small but exactly zero.

  \item \textbf{The Wiedemann--Franz ratio} is not an accident.  It is the
    universal consequence of the single phase-lock network carrying both
    charge and heat.  The Lorenz number is a fixed ratio of coupling
    constants ($\kB$ vs.\ $e$), independent of the particular conductor.
\end{itemize}

    Chapter~\ref{ch:transport}, Consequence~\ref{cons:wiedemann}.
\end{itemize}

Electrical transport is therefore not a separate domain of physics; it is
the categorical propagation formalism of bounded phase space applied to
electrons in a periodic potential.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=crownblue!5, colframe=crownblue!60!black,
                  title={\textbf{Chapter 18 Summary}}]
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Electric current is categorical state propagation through a
    phase-lock network.  The signal travels at $v_{\mathrm{signal}} \sim
    10^8$~m/s; carriers drift at $v_{\mathrm{drift}} \sim 10^{-4}$~m/s.
    The twelve-order-of-magnitude ratio is a theorem
    (Consequence~\ref{cons:newtonscradle}), not a paradox.

  \item Ohm's law $V = IR$ is proved from partition lag: $R = m_e L/(n_e
    e^2 A\tau_s)$ and $\sigma = n_e e^2\tau_s/m_e$
    (Consequence~\ref{cons:ohmslaw}).

  \item Kirchhoff's current law is categorical state conservation at
    junctions (Consequence~\ref{cons:kcl}).  Kirchhoff's voltage law is the
    single-valuedness of the S-potential (Consequence~\ref{cons:kvl}).

  \item The universal resistivity formula
    $\rho = (n_e e^2)^{-1}\sum_{ij}\taup^{(ij)}g_{ij}$ provides the
    microscopic theory (Consequence~\ref{cons:uniresist}).  Matthiessen's
    rule is its immediate consequence (Consequence~\ref{cons:matthiessen}).

  \item Resistivity is linear in $T$ for $T > \Theta_D$ and follows
    $\rho \propto T^5$ for $T \ll \Theta_D$
    (Consequence~\ref{cons:lineartdep}).  Residual resistivity measures
    sample impurity.

  \item Superconductivity is partition extinction: coupling collapse
    $g \to 0$ empties the transport sum and gives $\rho = 0$ exactly
    (Consequence~\ref{cons:superconductivity}).

  \item The Wiedemann--Franz law $\kappa/(\sigma T) = L_0$ follows from
    both charge and heat propagating through the same partition network
    (Consequence~\ref{cons:wiedemann}).

  \item All results follow from the single axiom: bounded phase space
    limits electron states to the Brillouin zone, forces categorical
    transitions to carry a partition lag $\taup$, and makes every
    transport coefficient a weighted sum over those lags.
\end{itemize}
